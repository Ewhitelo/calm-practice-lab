30-second breathing

Three slow breaths. Breathe in for 4 seconds, out for 6. The longer exhale helps your body settle. Use it before an exam, a presentation, or a hard conversation.

Why this works

Slow breathing with a longer exhale is a common way to bring the body out of its stress response. It is one route to what Herbert Benson called the Relaxation Response. Results vary from person to person, and the skill improves with repetition.

Progressive muscle relaxation

Each muscle group lights up on the body first, so you can get ready. Then tense it for 5 seconds and let go for 10. Notice the difference between tight and loose.

Why this works

Progressive muscle relaxation was developed by Edmund Jacobson. Learning to tell tension from relaxation makes it easier to catch stress in your body early. Tense gently, about 70 percent effort. Skip or only imagine tensing any area that is injured or painful.

Body scan

Move your attention slowly from your feet to the top of your head. You are not trying to change anything. Just notice warmth, pressure, tingling, tightness, or nothing at all.

Why this works

A body scan is a mindfulness practice that trains steady, non-judging attention to physical sensation. If your mind wanders, which it will, notice that and gently return to the area on the screen.

Grounding: 5-4-3-2-1

When your mind is racing, bring your attention back to the room using your five senses. Name 5 things you can see, 4 you can feel, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, and 1 you can taste. Tap each time you notice one.

Why this works

Grounding techniques shift attention from worried thoughts to what is happening around you right now. Naming sensory details, out loud or silently, gives your mind a simple job and can lower the intensity of anxious feelings. Go at your own pace.

Cognitive restructuring

Test whether a stressful thought is accurate. You will write the thought down, weigh the evidence, spot thinking traps, and build a more balanced thought. Nothing you type is saved or sent anywhere.

Why this works

In cognitive-behavioral therapy, the way you interpret a situation shapes how you feel. A thought record, introduced by Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy tradition, slows that process down so you can check the thought against the facts. Restructuring asks: is this thought true? Reframing, in the next tab, asks: is there another useful way to look at it?

Cognitive reframing

Reframing looks at a real situation from a more useful angle. First practice spotting good reframes. Then try one on a stressor of your own.

Why this works

The same event can mean different things depending on the frame around it. A good reframe does not deny the problem. It adds perspective that makes the next step clearer. The practice cards show the difference between a balanced reframe and forced positivity.
